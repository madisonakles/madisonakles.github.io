Background Summary

Digital spaces generate implicit virtual communication norms that regulate user interaction (Postmes et al., 2000). Masked hostility utilizes "weaponized politeness" or polite incongruence to deliver harsh critiques while buffering the commenter's social image (Brown & Levinson, 1987; Taylor, 2015). While "toxic positivity" and traditional cyberbullying are highly researched (Underwood & Ehrenreich, 2017; Lew & Flanagin, 2025), hyper-positive sarcasm—colloquially termed "glazing"—remains unexamined.

Core Study Hypotheses

The underlying study evaluates youth responses (ages 13–24) exposed to simulated TikTok layouts across three experimental assignments: Glazing (polite surface hostility), Direct Hostility, and Control.

H1 (Perception Gap): Participants in the Glazing condition will rate comments as significantly "nicer" and less punishable than those in the Direct Hate condition ($p < .05$). Because language markers mimic prosocial patterns, standard bystander intervention triggers are not activated (Koehler, 2018).

H2 (Relational Sting): Self-reported emotional distress will show no significant difference between the Glazing and Direct Hostility groups. This supports the assertion that subtle relational aggression is equally toxic, threatening target inclusion without presenting a transparent path for self-defense.

Methodology & System Gaps

The research parses tracking metrics (dwell-times, structural scale arrays, and behavioral triggers) to counter a vital vulnerability in automated platform safety: modern keyword-detection models routinely bypass weaponized civility, leaving vulnerable creators unprotected against context-coded toxicity.
