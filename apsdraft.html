Background & Theoretical Framework

Social media comment sections are dense hubs of complex interpersonal interaction. Over time, these digital micro-cultures develop distinct, socially constructed virtual communication norms that implicitly regulate user behavior and group dynamics (Postmes et al., 2000).

Online passive-aggressiveness closely mirrors traditional, culturally embedded regional norms. A prime example is the classic Southern American idiom "bless your heart"—a phrase that leverages a surface-level veneer of sympathy to mask a patronizing, backhanded critique. Employing polite incongruence allows speakers to deliver harsh critiques while protecting their own social image from appearing outwardly aggressive (Brown & Levinson, 1987).

Literature exists on the effects of “toxic positivity” on social media (Lew & Flanagin, 2025) and demonstrates that sarcastic criticism is overall rated as less negative than literal criticism (Filik et al., 2016). However, there is a growing trend of digital hyperpositivity—colloquially termed “glazing”—that remains largely unexamined. Masked hostility utilizes "weaponized politeness" to deliver face-threatening acts while maintaining the social protections of prosocial behavior (Taylor, 2015; Dynel, 2016).

Traditional cyberbullying research has historically prioritized overt toxicity (e.g., flaming or threats), yet adolescent social dynamics are increasingly defined by indirect aggression and social manipulation (Underwood & Ehrenreich, 2017). Research on relational aggression suggests that these subtle slights can be more distressing than direct insults because they threaten a victim’s social inclusion without offering a clear path for defense. Because external observers—and automated moderation systems—perceive the surface-level language as positive, the social norms that typically trigger bystander intervention are never activated (Koehler, 2018).

The Proposed Study

This framework investigates online “glazing”—overcompensatory, hyper-positive praise or flattery that feels forced, unearned, or overtly sarcastic. Commenters are potentially over-positive because they anticipate a wave of hateful or toxic comments on a post. Utilizing a preregistered, between-subjects experimental design targeting approximately 300 participants (ages 13–24), users are exposed to fictional, TikTok-style comment threads across three randomly assigned conditions:

  • Condition 1 (Glazing): Comment threads utilizing polite surface language to convey hostility (e.g., backhanded compliments).
  • Condition 2 (Direct Hostility): Explicitly negative, overt insults derived from common real-world toxicity streams.
  • Condition 3 (Control): Neutral, standard interactions established via pilot testing baseline limits.

Hypotheses & Primary Outcome Measures

H1: Participants in the Glazing condition will rate the comments as significantly "nicer" and less punishable than those in the Direct Hate condition (p < .05).
H2: There will be no significant difference in self-reported emotional distress between the Glazing and Direct Hate groups, supporting the hypothesis that the "sting" of hostility is not dulled by polite phrasing.

To test this, the simulation parses tracked behavioral and evaluative metrics: interaction time-deltas, selection frequency of interactive elements (likes/dislikes), structural perception arrays (severity, hatred vs politeness/niceness), and emotional outcomes using custom subscales.

Platform Infrastructure Implications

The practically targeted objective exposes critical vulnerabilities in automated content evaluation. Current AI moderation tools rely heavily on keyword or expression blacklists (e.g., profanity filters) to identify threats. When weaponized civility matches standard prosocial patterns, it lowers bystander intervention while leaving target subjects socially isolated. Platform data systems must adjust toward context-aware architectures engineered to process multi-layered contextual intent over simple lexical pattern mapping.
